5-minute AI safety start

The teacher-in-the-loop rule AI drafts. You decide. Every output must be reviewed before it reaches a student.

Do not enter student personal information

Public AI tools like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are not approved school systems. Never paste student names, IDs, grades, IEP details, behavior notes, contact info, or anything that identifies a child. Use general non-identifying context only.

Always verify the output

AI can sound confident and still be wrong. Check facts, math, reading levels, citations, and any historical or scientific claims before classroom use.

Follow your school and district policy

Rules differ by district, state, and program. Before you use AI for grading, parent communication, or anything student-facing, check your local AI policy and FERPA expectations.

Not for high-stakes decisions Do not use AI output alone for final grades, retention, special education determinations, discipline, or college recommendations.

Chapter 1 - One prompt shape Core skill

One prompt shape that works every time.

Most weak prompts skip context. Strong prompts tell the AI who it is helping you as, what to produce, your grade and goal, the format you want, your limits, and what to double-check. Use this shape every time.

Tell the AI…Include
Who it's helpingWho the AI is acting as. Example: an experienced Grade 5 math teaching assistant.
What to makeWhat you want it to produce. Lesson, quiz, exit ticket, feedback, etc.
Your classroomGrade, topic, learning goal, time available, class needs - without student personal data.
The formatThe exact shape you want: sections, length, bullets, table, rubric.
Your limitsAge-appropriate, classroom materials only, no outside tools, vocabulary level.
Before you use itAsk the AI to flag anything you should verify before teaching.

Bad vs good

Bad prompt
Make a lesson about fractions.
Good prompt
You are an experienced Grade 5 math teaching assistant. Create a 45-minute lesson on adding unlike fractions. Students know equivalent fractions but struggle with common denominators. Give me: objective, warm-up, instruction, guided practice, exit ticket. Classroom materials only. Flag anything I should verify.

Quick check before you paste

  • Did I say who the AI is helping me as?
  • Did I name what I need clearly?
  • Did I add grade, topic, and goal?
  • Did I describe the format I want?
  • Did I list limits my classroom requires?
  • Did I ask what to verify before use?
  • Did I avoid any student personal data?

Chapter 3 - Lesson planning prompts Plan in 5 min

Lesson planning pack

Same idea as #1 on page 4 — with more detail.

Three lesson templates: a 30-minute mini-lesson, a 45-minute full lesson, and a differentiated lesson. Replace the brackets, run the prompt, then verify and revise.

30-minute mini-lesson

You are an experienced Grade [N] [subject] teaching assistant. Build a 30-minute mini-lesson on [topic]. Students [what they know / need]. Time: 30 minutes. Give me: objective, 5-min warm-up, 10-min instruction, 10-min practice, 5-min exit ticket, and a 1-line teacher reminder. Classroom materials only. No outside tools. Flag any facts I should verify before teaching.

45-minute full lesson

You are an experienced Grade [N] [subject] teaching assistant. Build a 45-minute lesson on [topic] with learning goal: [goal]. Mixed-readiness class. Vocabulary support needed for [topic]. Give me: objective, warm-up, vocabulary preview, direct instruction, guided practice, independent task, exit ticket, teacher checklist. Grade-appropriate reading level. Include one misconception check. Flag any unfamiliar terms students may not understand.

Differentiated lesson (one objective, three pathways)

You are a differentiated instruction assistant. Build a lesson on [topic] for Grade [N] with one shared objective and three pathways. Some students need vocabulary and step-by-step support. Some are ready for extension. Give me: shared objective, warm-up, support pathway with scaffolds, core pathway, stretch pathway with extension thinking, and a single exit ticket that works for all three. Same objective, different supports. No labels on the student handout. Flag any pathway that drifts from the shared objective.

Chapter 4 - Assessment and exit tickets Check learning

Build quick, aligned assessments

Same idea as #2 and #3 on page 4 — with more detail.

Match every assessment item to the lesson objective. AI is great for drafting items and rubrics; you check alignment, fairness, and the answer key.

Quick quiz prompt

You are an assessment design assistant. Build a 10-question quiz for Grade [N] on [topic], aligned to objective: [objective]. Give me: 7 multiple choice (4 options, 1 correct), 2 short answer, 1 open-ended. Include an answer key and short scoring criteria for the open items. Grade-appropriate language. Avoid trick questions. Distractors should reflect common misconceptions. Flag any item that does not align to the objective.

Exit ticket prompt

You are an exit-ticket designer. Write 3 exit-ticket questions for Grade [N] checking understanding of [concept]. Give me three short questions, each designed to surface a different specific misconception. Include the misconception each one targets. 5 minutes or less to complete. No graded points. Note what next-day reteach is needed if students miss each item.

Simple rubric template

Criterion4 - Strong3 - Proficient2 - Developing1 - Beginning
AccuracyComplete and correctMostly correctPartly correctLimited
EvidenceStrong evidenceSome evidenceMinimal evidenceNo clear evidence
ExplanationClear reasoningMostly clearUnclearMissing
Always verify the answer key. AI can produce plausible-but-wrong answers, especially for math, science, and history. Solve each item yourself or check with a trusted source before printing.

Chapter 5 - Differentiation prompts Reach more students

One objective, three pathways

Same idea as #4 on page 4 — with more detail.

AI is most useful when you need fast variations - support, core, and stretch - that still hit the same learning goal.

Three-level task prompt

You are a differentiation assistant. Generate three versions of the same task on [topic] for Grade [N]. Support version with sentence frames and step-by-step hints, core version for whole class, stretch version with one extension question that pushes higher-order thinking. Same objective and time limit across all three. No labels visible on student copies. Verify all three pathways measure the same objective.

Reading-level adjustment prompt

You are a reading-level adjustment assistant. Rewrite the passage below at a Grade [N] reading level without changing meaning. Give me: rewritten passage, glossary of 3-5 key terms with one-line definitions, and 2 comprehension questions. Keep proper nouns and key academic vocabulary; explain them inline. Passage: [paste passage]

Multilingual learner support prompt

You are a support assistant for multilingual learners. Create a vocabulary pre-teach for [topic] in Grade [N]. Give me 6 key terms with kid-friendly definitions, example sentences, and a simple visual cue idea for each. Plain English. No idioms. No cultural assumptions.
Privacy reminder. Do not paste IEPs, 504 plans, disability labels, or student names. Use general phrasing like "some students need vocabulary support" or "a few students need extension."

Chapter 6 - Feedback prompts Faster, clearer

Write better feedback in less time

Same idea as #6 on page 4 — with more detail.

AI is good at turning vague comments into specific, kind, actionable feedback. You still own the judgment - never paste student work that includes names or identifiers.

Feedback rewrite prompt

You are a feedback writing assistant for K-12 teachers. Rewrite the teacher comment below to be specific, kind, and actionable for a Grade [N] student. One short paragraph using the pattern: strength, next step, suggestion. Plain language. No labels, no judgment of the student as a person, no academic jargon. Draft: [paste your draft feedback - no student name]

Student-friendly feedback template

You did well with [specific strength]. Your next step is [specific improvement]. Try [one concrete action]. I will look for this in your next [task].

Whole-class feedback prompt

You are a whole-class feedback assistant. Based on these patterns I observed, draft whole-class feedback I can read aloud at the start of the next lesson. Patterns: [describe 2-3 patterns - e.g., "many students wrote strong claims but weak evidence"] Give me: two strengths to celebrate, one shared next step, and one quick re-teach idea. No student names. No comparisons between students.

Feedback quality checklist

  • Specific - it points to something in the work.
  • Actionable - the student knows what to do next.
  • Kind - it respects the student as a learner.
  • Aligned - it ties back to the lesson objective.
  • Bias-free - it judges the work, not the person.

Chapter 7 - AI output verification Print and tape this

Before you use AI output with students

Print this page. Tape it next to your planning workspace. Run every AI output through these checks before it reaches a student.

The 7-point verification checklist

  • Accurate. I solved or looked up every fact, calculation, or claim.
  • Aligned. The content matches my learning objective for this lesson.
  • Age-appropriate. Reading level, examples, and references fit the grade.
  • Bias-aware. I checked for cultural, racial, gender, and stereotype risks.
  • Private. No student personal data was put into the AI tool.
  • Policy-compliant. This use is allowed under my school and district AI policy.
  • Teacher-approved. I reviewed and edited the final version before printing.

Red flags - revise or discard

  • Confident-sounding facts you cannot verify.
  • Citations, URLs, or sources that do not exist.
  • Math answers that you did not check yourself.
  • Historical claims with specific dates, names, or quotes you have not verified.
  • Generic examples that ignore your class context.
  • Content that stereotypes any group of students.

If something is wrong

Three-step fix.
  1. Tell the AI exactly what is wrong and ask it to revise.
  2. Re-check the revision against this checklist.
  3. If two revisions do not fix it, write that part yourself.

Chapter 8 - Quick glossary and beginner mistakes Reference

Plain-English AI glossary

Generative AI
Software that produces text, images, or other content from a prompt and learned patterns.
Prompt
The instruction you give the AI. Better context yields better output.
Hallucination
When AI invents facts, sources, or quotes that look real but are not.
Bias
Unfair patterns in AI output that reflect uneven training data.
Verification
Your check that the output is accurate, age-appropriate, and aligned.
PII
Personally identifiable information. Never put student PII into AI tools.

Common beginner mistakes

MistakeWhy it hurtsFix
Asking vague questionsYou get vague output.Add grade, goal, and output format.

How this guide works Read first

Pedagogy first. AI second.

AI accelerates drafting, variation, sequencing, and feedback cycles. Without learning objectives, taxonomy alignment, and human governance, AI mostly produces attractive but shallow materials. This guide is built so AI strengthens your existing instructional design system, not the other way around.

What AI speeds up

  • Lesson and assessment drafts compress from hours to minutes.
  • Differentiated variants of the same task become trivial to produce.
  • Rubrics, exemplars, and misconception checks can be generated on demand.
  • Teachers gain time - if and only if the materials are reviewable and standards-aligned.

What goes wrong without a clear objective

  • Without a clear objective, AI optimizes for plausible-looking content.
  • Without assessment alignment, "engaging" lessons drift away from evidence of learning.
  • Without taxonomy guidance, AI defaults to recall-level tasks.
  • Without UDL, AI tends to produce one pathway and label it as "the lesson."

Why naming cognitive demand helps

When prompts name the cognitive demand explicitly - Bloom level, DOK level, or SOLO stage - AI output shifts from generic to instructionally meaningful. Taxonomy is not decoration. It is a quality lever.

Why adoption needs structure

  • Tool training without workflow integration creates short-lived enthusiasm.
  • Policy ambiguity stalls adoption faster than any technical issue.
  • Without QA rubrics, AI-supported materials accumulate quiet inconsistencies.
  • Coaching beats one-off PD by a wide margin.

Chapter 1.1 - Instructional design foundations Framework map

Six frameworks - when to use each

Each framework solves a different problem. Use them in combination, not in competition.

FrameworkBest atUse when
Backward DesignAligning desired results, evidence, and instruction.Designing or auditing any unit or course.
Bloom's TaxonomyNaming cognitive action with action verbs.Writing objectives, varying task difficulty intentionally.
Webb's DOKDescribing cognitive complexity.Auditing assessments and tasks for depth, not just topic coverage.
UDLRemoving access barriers.Designing engagement, representation, and action/expression options up front.
SOLO TaxonomyDescribing increasing structural complexity of understanding.Designing rubrics and progressions of understanding.
Competency-basedDefining observable mastery.Programs that need evidence of mastery beyond seat time.

Common misuses

  • Treating Bloom as difficulty. Bloom describes cognitive type, not how hard a question is to answer.
  • Confusing DOK with verbs. A task is DOK 3 because of the thinking required, not because it uses the word "analyze."
  • Adding UDL as decoration. UDL must shape design from the start, not arrive as accommodations after the fact.
  • Writing rubrics after misaligned tasks. Score the wrong thing well and it is still wrong.
  • Asking AI for "engaging lessons" before defining evidence of learning.
One rule. Never adopt a framework just to look rigorous. Adopt one because it answers a question your team is actually trying to answer.

Chapter 2.1 - Master prompt template Master prompt

The master prompt for coaches

This template is the single prompt structure you can extend into every instructional design conversation with AI. It mirrors backward design, names cognitive demand, and forces explicit success criteria.

Role: You are an instructional design assistant supporting U.S. K-12 curriculum work. You are helping a curriculum lead or instructional coach design K-12 materials. Goal: [Design / improve / audit] instructional materials for [unit / lesson / assessment]. Learner profile: Grade [N], subject [subject], readiness [brief], language needs [brief], accessibility needs [brief]. No student PII. Framework: Use [Backward Design / Bloom / DOK / UDL / SOLO / competency]. Name the cognitive demand explicitly. Learning objective: [single observable statement aligned to standard] Cognitive demand: [Bloom level and DOK level] Evidence of learning: [what students will produce that proves the objective is met] Assessment type: [formative / summative / performance] Constraints: [time, resources, materials, language level, accessibility] Output format: [exact shape: sections, tables, rubric, length] Quality criteria: Each output element must align to the objective. Include misconception checks. Use UDL multiple-means principles. Flag every claim that needs human verification. Ask before assuming: List the assumptions you are making and ask up to 3 clarifying questions before producing the final output.

How to extend the master prompt

  • For a unit plan: add "Sequence of 6 lessons with formative checkpoints."
  • For a rubric: add "Generate a 4-level rubric with descriptors using SOLO complexity language."
  • For PD design: add "Produce facilitator notes, time blocks, and an evaluation method."
  • For an audit: add "Score this material against the audit rubric in Chapter 3."

Chapter 2.2 - Backward design prompt system UbD pipeline

From desired results to learning plan

Backward design has three stages: identify desired results, determine acceptable evidence, plan learning experiences. Use these three prompts in sequence. Do not let AI skip Stage 2.

Stage 1 - Desired results

Role: You are an instructional design assistant using Wiggins and McTighe's Understanding by Design. Task: Draft the desired-results section for a Grade [N] [subject] unit on [topic]. Output: Enduring understanding (1 sentence), essential questions (3), knowledge students will acquire (5 items), skills students will develop (5 items), and the standard(s) this addresses. Quality check: Each item must be defensible and assessable.

Stage 2 - Evidence of learning

Role: Same as Stage 1. Task: Define acceptable evidence for the desired results from Stage 1. Output: One performance task using the GRASPS pattern (Goal, Role, Audience, Situation, Product, Standards), plus 3 supporting assessments and a rubric outline with criteria. Quality check: Each piece of evidence must directly correspond to an item from Stage 1.

Stage 3 - Learning plan

Role: Same as Stage 1. Task: Build a learning plan using the WHERETO framework (Where headed, Hook, Equip, Rethink, Evaluate, Tailor, Organize). Output: A sequence of [N] lessons mapped to each WHERETO element, with one formative checkpoint per lesson. Quality check: Every lesson must move evidence forward.
Anti-pattern. Skipping Stage 2 is the most common UbD failure with or without AI. Lessons get built before evidence is defined and the result is engaging-but-misaligned material. Stop and run Stage 2 explicitly.

Chapter 2.3 - UDL prompt system Access-first design

Universal Design for Learning - by design, not by accommodation

UDL has three principles: multiple means of engagement, representation, and action/expression. Use these three prompts when designing the lesson itself, not as bolt-ons.

Engagement prompt

Role: You are a UDL design assistant. Task: For a Grade [N] lesson on [topic], generate three engagement options that recruit interest, sustain effort, and support self-regulation. Output: Three options labeled by purpose, each with a concrete classroom activity and a why-it-works note. Constraints: Same learning objective across all three. No labels visible on student handouts.

Representation prompt

Role: Same as above. Task: For the same lesson, generate three representations of the core content. Output: A textual explanation, a visual or diagrammatic representation, and a real-world example. Add brief notes about vocabulary support. Constraints: All three representations must teach the same concept at the same cognitive level.

Action and expression prompt

Role: Same as above. Task: For the same lesson, design three ways students can demonstrate learning. Output: A written option, a verbal or performance option, and a visual or modeled option. Provide aligned rubric criteria that work across all three. Constraints: Same rubric, same objective, equivalent rigor across modes.
UDL audit tip. If your lesson has only one engagement, one representation, and one expression option, you have a single-pathway lesson - not a UDL lesson. Use these three prompts to expand before printing materials.

Chapter 2.4 - Differentiation transformations Lift cognitive demand

Transform low-level tasks into higher-order tasks

Most existing materials sit at DOK 1-2. You can lift tasks to DOK 3-4 with AI - if you ask explicitly. Use the transformation prompt below.

The transformation prompt

Role: You are an instructional design transformation assistant. Task: Take the low-level task below and produce three transformations that raise cognitive demand without changing the topic. Output: The original task, then DOK 2, DOK 3, and DOK 4 versions. For each, name the cognitive shift and the Bloom level. Include a one-line rubric criterion. Constraints: Keep the topic and approximate time. Do not add new content; lift the thinking. Original task: [paste current task]

Worked example - Grade 8 ELA

LevelCognitive shiftTask
OriginalRecallList three character traits of the protagonist.
DOK 2Skill with conceptual understandingChoose three traits and cite one piece of textual evidence for each.
DOK 3Reasoning with evidenceArgue which trait most drives the character's central conflict, using two pieces of evidence.
DOK 4Extended thinking across sourcesCompare the protagonist to a real or historical figure. Build an evidence-based claim about how trait-driven conflict shapes outcomes in both lives.
Avoid verb-only "rigor." Asking students to "analyze" a list does not produce DOK 3 thinking. The transformation must change the cognitive work, not just the verb.

Chapter 3.1 - Curriculum audit framework Scoring 1-4

Curriculum and lesson audit

Use one rubric per job - don't score everything every time. Score each dimension from 1 to 4, then apply the improvement protocol.

DimensionWhat to look forScore
Objective clarityOne observable statement, aligned to a standard.__
Taxonomy alignmentBloom and DOK levels match the objective.__
Assessment alignmentEvery item measures the stated objective.__
Cognitive depthTasks operate at the intended DOK level.__
Student engagementMultiple means of engagement present by design.__
AccessibilityReading level, formatting, and supports work for the intended learners.__
DifferentiationSupport, core, and stretch pathways with the same objective.__
SafetyNo student PII; AI claims are verified.__
BiasExamples and language avoid stereotype and exclusion.__
Teacher usabilityMaterials are classroom-ready without major rewriting.__

Scoring guide

4 - Ready to use as is.
3 - Usable with minor revision.
2 - Needs revision before classroom use.
1 - Not ready; redesign required.

Red flags - automatically score 1

  • No measurable objective stated.
  • Activity is fun but unrelated to the stated objective.
  • Assessment tests recall when the objective requires analysis or evaluation.
  • AI output includes facts, sources, or quotes that cannot be verified.
  • Student PII appears in any materials.
  • Materials assume one learning pathway and call it universal.
  • Rubric criteria are vague or unconnected to the work.

Chapter 4.4 - PD model: 4-week implementation Adoption arc

4-week implementation arc

One-day PD does not change practice. This 4-week arc is the minimum durable adoption pattern we recommend. Pair each week with one coaching touchpoint per teacher.

WeekFocusTeacher output
Week 1Safe use and prompt basics.One verified prompt and one verification log.
Week 2Objectives and assessment alignment.One aligned objective + formative checkpoint pair.
Week 3Differentiation and UDL.One lesson with three pathways and matching rubric.
Week 4Peer review and evidence of impact.Rubric-scored materials and short reflection.

Coaching pattern (per teacher, per week)

  1. Plan together (15 min). Pick the lesson, decide the AI move.
  2. Build (asynchronous, 30 min). Teacher drafts with AI; coach is available.
  3. Review (15 min). Score with the lesson review rubric.
  4. Teach. Teacher delivers the lesson.
  5. Debrief (15 min). What worked, one shift for next time.

Trainer evaluation methods

  • Pre/post teacher confidence survey (5 Likert items).
  • Materials quality - count of items scoring 3 or 4 on the rubric.
  • Adoption depth - rolling count of teachers active in the prompt bank.
  • Safety - zero privacy incidents reported across the month.

Chapter 6.1 - QA and governance Human in the loop

Human validation workflow

Every AI-supported material that reaches a student should pass through this workflow. Build it into your team norms, not into individual willpower.

1

Generate

Use the master prompt with framework named.
→
2

Fact check

Verify claims, math, citations, dates, quotes.
→
3

Align

Confirm task-objective-assessment alignment.
4

Access

Apply UDL check and reading-level review.
→
5

Privacy

Confirm no PII; check policy compliance.
→
6

Approve

Teacher signature; log version in shared bank.

Governance rules

  • No student PII in any public AI tool.
  • Teacher validates every output before classroom use.
  • AI is never the sole basis for grading, retention, discipline, special education determinations, or college recommendations.
  • District policy is checked before each new use case is launched.
  • Bias and hallucination checks are required for any student-facing content.
  • Versioned documentation is retained for shared curriculum assets.
Governance is a system, not a slogan. Make the workflow the path of least resistance. If verification takes longer than reuse, teachers will quietly drop it.

Chapter 7.1 - Case study: elementary adoption Case study

Case study 1 - Grade 4 team adoption

A typical Grade 4 team — adapt these steps to your school.

Context

A K-5 elementary school with a six-teacher Grade 4 team. Mixed comfort with technology. Limited prep time. District has just approved use of one general-purpose AI tool.

Problem

Teachers are creating exit tickets and vocabulary supports inconsistently. Some students get rich vocabulary pre-teaching; others get none. Planning time is the bottleneck.

AI-supported solution

  • Adopt the master prompt for vocabulary pre-teach and exit tickets.
  • Run a 90-minute workshop followed by a 4-week implementation arc.
  • Coach observes one Grade 4 lesson per teacher in week 3.
  • All prompts pass through the prompt quality rubric before joining the team bank.

Risks anticipated

  • Over-reliance on AI vocabulary lists without teacher edits.
  • Uneven prompt quality across the team.
  • Privacy edge cases when describing student needs.

Outcome indicators

  • Faster planning - measured by self-report at week 2 and week 4.
  • Vocabulary pre-teach now present in every Grade 4 lesson packet.
  • Exit-ticket-based reteach decisions made by all six teachers.
  • Zero privacy incidents.

What they created

  • Grade 4 prompt bank (vocabulary, exit tickets, scaffolds).
  • Lesson review rubric scoring log.
  • Coaching notes per teacher.

Chapter 7.2 - Case study: high school department Case study

Case study 2 - High school biology department

A typical high school department — adapt these steps to your school.

Context

An eight-teacher biology department serving grades 9-12. The team has noticed that unit assessments lean heavily on recall. They want to lift cognitive demand without rewriting everything from scratch.

Problem

Assessments and tasks largely sit at DOK 1-2. Students do well on recall but struggle on inference, explanation, and evidence-based argument. Time to redesign is scarce.

AI-supported solution

  • Use the differentiation transformation prompt to lift existing tasks to DOK 3 and DOK 4.
  • Build a shared misconception checklist for the unit.
  • Calibrate scoring with the lesson review rubric during a half-day PD.
  • Pair each teacher with a peer reviewer for the first three weeks.

Risks anticipated

  • AI-generated science explanations that sound correct but are inaccurate.
  • Misconception lists that miss known student errors specific to local context.
  • Rigor inflation - tasks that look harder without actually testing deeper thinking.

Outcome indicators

  • Share of unit assessment items at DOK 3+ rises from baseline.
  • Peer review identifies and fixes a number of misalignment issues per teacher.
  • Teachers report higher-quality student discussion in observations.
  • Department leader sees fewer last-minute material changes mid-unit.

What they created

  • Transformed task bank by unit.
  • Shared misconception checklist.

Case study 3 - District PD rollout

A typical district cohort — adapt these steps to your context.

Context

A small district training 20 teachers across three schools as a first cohort, with the intent to scale district-wide the following semester.

Solution outline

  • Train instructional coaches first using the full-day model.
  • Run 90-minute workshops at each school, followed by 4-week implementation.
  • Publish a single AI use guide aligned to district policy and FERPA expectations.
  • Set up a shared prompt bank with version control and rubric-scored entries.

Risks and mitigations

  • Policy confusion - mitigate with a one-page decision tree and a single source of truth.
  • Inconsistent quality - mitigate with rubric calibration sessions monthly.
  • Tool churn - mitigate with tool-agnostic prompts.

What they created

  • District AI use guide.
  • PD slide deck and facilitator notes.
  • Lesson review rubric and prompt quality rubric.
  • Support calendar - office hours, coaching cycles, calibration meetings.
